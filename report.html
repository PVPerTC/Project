Research Question

As the years change, how do demographic factors (age and gender) and geographical location (areas) influence the overall mortality rates of heart disease in Scotland? The reason is that it is important to understand these differences in public health. By identifying which demographic groups are most vulnerable and which regions experience higher mortality rates, policymakers and healthcare providers can better allocate resources and design targeted interventions to reduce heart disease mortality across Scotland.

Limitations of the Simple Model

The simple linear regression plot shows a slight downward trend in CHD mortality rates between 2015 and 2023. However, the observed mortality rates vary considerably across Council Areas within the same year.

This suggests that year alone may not adequately explain the variation in mortality rates. Therefore, I choose to extend the model by including Council Area as an additional predictor.

Discussion and Limitations

Regression Results and Discussion

The simple linear regression model showed a weak relationship between year and CHD mortality rates, with an R-squared value of 0.017. This means that year alone explained only 1.7% of the variation in mortality rates across the selected Council Areas.

After adding Council Area to the multiple linear regression model, the R-squared increased to 0.743, and the adjusted R-squared increased from 0.006 to 0.711. The residual standard error also decreased from 32.986 to 17.796.

These results suggest that including geographical location substantially improved the model’s fit. The multiple regression plot also shows clear differences in predicted mortality rates between Council Areas, while the overall trend decreases over time.

However, the analysis only includes ten Council Areas selected based on their mortality rates. Therefore, the findings may not represent all areas in Scotland.

Data

The dataset for this investigation is the Scottish Heart Disease Statistics. I utilized the mortality data categorized by Council Areas and filtered for overall heart disease mortality. During the data wrangling process using the tidyverse package, I joined the raw mortality data with the Data Zone Codes dictionary to convert alphanumeric zone codes into readable Council Area names. I also removed missing values ‘NA’ to maintain data quality.

The final cleaned dataset focuses on four key variables for our investigation: 1. Year: The variable tracking mortality trends. 2. Gender: A variable differentiating male and female mortality. 3. Age_group: Demographic brackets (0-44 years, 45-64 years, 65-74 years, under 75 years and 75+ years). 4. Area: The Scottish Council Areas, which is necessary for regional comparison.
